Basic tissues in organs

Tissues can also form structures that are not usually thought of as organs. However, because they are formed by two or more tissues, they are truly organs. Examples shown here are bone, skin and blood vessels. 100x, 100x, 400x

Bone

Bone is an organ because it is composed of several types of connective tissue (the tissue bone, bone marrow, and a connective tissue proper covering), blood vessels (composed of epithelium and muscle), and nerves.

Skin

Skin is composed of epithelial tissues, forming a surface epidermis and deeper glands along with a connective tissue layer that forms the dermis. Blood vessels and nerves are also present.

Epithelium

Two of these organs have a free surface and, therefore, are lined by an epithelium. The free surface of the skin is the exterior surface of the body and the free surface of the artery is the lumen through which blood flows.

Connective tissue proper

Several types of connective tissue proper are found in these three organs, forming a covering for bone, the dermis of skin, the hypodermis beneath the skin, and part of the wall of the artery.

Muscle

Muscle is a frequent component of organs and forms a majority of the arterial wall. Muscle also forms an important component in the walls of other hollow organs, as seen in the previous image of the stomach. Smooth muscle is also present in the skin, where it is attached to hair follicles. Muscle is absent in bone, except in the walls of vessels supplying the bones.
